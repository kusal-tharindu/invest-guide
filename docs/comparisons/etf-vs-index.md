# ETF vs Index

!!! info "Draft by a learner"
    This is my current understanding. I will correct it as I learn more.

When I started, I kept seeing "S&P 500" and "S&P 500 ETF" used as if they were the same thing. They are not. This page explains the difference.

## The short answer

- An **index** is a list and a score. It measures how a group of investments is doing.
- An **ETF** is a product you can buy. Most ETFs try to copy an index.

You cannot buy an index directly. You buy an ETF that follows it.

## What is an index?

An index is a rulebook plus a number. The rulebook says which companies are included (for example, the 500 large US companies). The number goes up and down as the prices of those companies change.

Think of it as a **scoreboard**. It tells you how the group is performing. It holds no money and has no owner you can invest with.

Examples:

- **S&P 500**: large US companies.
- **MSCI World**: large and mid-size companies across developed countries.
- **FTSE All-World**: companies across developed and emerging markets.

## What is an ETF?

ETF stands for **Exchange-Traded Fund**. It is a fund that holds many investments inside it and is bought and sold on a stock exchange, like a share.

An ETF that follows an index tries to hold the same companies as that index. When the index goes up, the ETF should go up by about the same amount, minus costs.

Think of it as the **product built from the scoreboard's rulebook**.

## Side by side

| | Index | ETF |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A list of investments and a number | A fund you can buy |
| Can you buy it? | No | Yes, through a broker |
| Holds real investments? | No | Yes |
| Has costs? | No | Yes (the fund's yearly fee, called TER) |
| Who runs it? | An index provider (S&P, MSCI, FTSE) | A fund company (iShares, Vanguard, and others) |
| Example | S&P 500 | An ETF that tracks the S&P 500 |

## One index, many ETFs

Several fund companies can build an ETF on the same index. Each one is a separate product with its own fee, size, and structure.

For example, more than one company offers an ETF that follows the S&P 500. They aim for the same result, but they can differ in:

- **Cost.** The yearly fee (TER).
- **Distribution.** Some pay out dividends, and some reinvest them for you.
- **Tracking.** How closely the ETF follows the index. The gap is called tracking difference.
- **Domicile.** The country where the fund is registered. This can affect tax.

!!! note "Why this matters for me"
    Because of domicile and tax rules, the same index can be a good choice in one country and a poor choice in another. I will cover this in the [By Location & Tax](../by-location/index.md) section.

## ETF vs index fund

A related term is **index fund**. It is also a fund that follows an index, but it is usually bought directly from the fund company and priced once a day. An ETF trades on an exchange all day.

Both aim to follow an index. The main difference is how you buy and sell them. I'll write a separate article on this.

## What I took from this

1. An index is a measurement. An ETF is something you buy.
2. Many ETFs can follow the same index.
3. When comparing ETFs on the same index, look at cost, distribution type, tracking, and domicile.
